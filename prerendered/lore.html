    01The loss
    Why it exists

    Not retrieval, elicitation.

    Every senior who retires takes decades of contextual knowledge with them: the exceptions, the quirks of one specific airframe, the patterns a manual cannot hold because nobody thought to write them down.

    The tools that exist do on explicit documents: manuals, standard procedures, service bulletins. Zymbly, LexX, AWS Q. They retrieve what somebody already put in. None of them capture what a senior never thought to write.

    That is the gap. Not retrieval, elicitation.

    What is missing is , the part of an trade that lives in the people doing it.

    02Three modes

    Capture, query, log. All by voice.

    • mic

      Capture

      The senior speaks.

      After an intervention, the senior debriefs by voice. Lore interviews back: asks follow-ups, flags what is ambiguous, and files the knowledge against the airframe, the component and the conditions it applies to.

    • hearing

      Query

      The junior asks.

      Hands inside the machine, a question out loud. The answer arrives in three layers, in this order: the relevant excerpt, then the senior's oral knowledge attributed by name and month, then this specific aircraft's own history.

    • edit_note

      Log

      The work is recorded.

      "Log: N1 vibration 2.4 units, ambient 6°C, no escalation." Straight into the aircraft's memory, spoken, no keyboard.

    Rule number one: Lore never contradicts a procedure. It completes it.

    03The hangar, 23:00

    The hangar, 23:00.

    Night shift. Thomas is two years in, working a scheduled check on F-GKXA. He finds a vibration that is not on the job card.

    There are other people in the hangar. None of them worked this airframe for twenty-six years. Marc did, and Marc retired in March.

    Thomas

    I'm on F-GKXA, CFM56-5B, fan section. Unusual low-frequency vibration on the N1 shaft. It's not in the job card. What do I know about this?

    Lore

    AMM 72-21-00 requires escalation above 4 units. Marc Delaunay noted in October that F-GKXA specifically shows a harmonic resonance between 2 and 3 units below 8°C, a known characteristic of this airframe, not a defect. He recommended logging it and monitoring across the next cycles before escalating. Always verify the AMM procedure before intervening.

    Thomas

    Logging: vibration at 2.4 units, temperature 6°C, no escalation per Marc's note. Add this to F-GKXA's memory.

    Lore

    Logged. F-GKXA memory updated.

    Marc has been retired for four months. He answered anyway.

    04Same thesis, one industry over
    The thread

    The same thesis as Aetherix, one industry over.

    What separates them is the cost of being wrong. In hospitality it compounds quietly: food thrown away, a shift overstaffed while another runs short, a new manager taking a year to learn what the last one knew, a guest who meets the consequences before anyone in the building does. In maintenance it does not compound. A wrong figure touches on the first occurrence.

    Hospitality

    A manager resigns.

    Aetherix exists because most hotels lose critical knowledge every time a manager leaves. A bad forecast wastes food and overstaffs a Tuesday.

    05The hazard

    The hazard nobody else has.

    A junior with a manual has the limits but not the judgement. A junior with a senior on shift has both, which is why shops have always run that way.

    Lore's bet is that the second half survives the person. What it surfaces is a named technician's judgement about a specific airframe, in the same breath as the procedure that governs it, never instead of it.

    That is also the part that has to be engineered rather than promised. Oral knowledge is trusted and sits partly outside the manual, so the only acceptable design is one where the procedure always wins and the judgement always arrives attributed.

    It is not hypothetical. It is already in the demo data, where a senior's judgement sits next to the .

    06What makes an answer admissible

    What makes an answer admissible.

    Two rules, neither designed in advance. They are not a defence against a bad model, they are the condition under which used knowledge can be counted at all: a system that invents figures or manufactures agreement would feed the loop noise and call it evidence.

    The refusal contract.

    Eight rules, written because "say so honestly" was not enough: early on the model refused correctly and then volunteered "36 fan blades" anyway, and elsewhere answered about one tail number using a different aircraft's borescope record. Honesty about the gap did not stop it filling the gap. The rules now say it plainly: no figure absent from the sources, no other aircraft's record, no general engine knowledge, no unnamed source, no claimed consensus.

    The closing sentence is enforced in code, not in the prompt.

    Every maintenance answer ends with "Always verify the AMM procedure before intervening." Asked to close with it and also ask a follow-up, the model reliably ordered them the wrong way round, so the reminder read as if it applied to the pleasantry. The safety documentation calls that sentence non-removable, which is a claim about code, so code now holds it.

    07How we know it holds

    How we know it holds.

    The failure mode here is not a crash. It is a syntactically perfect answer that gives a technician the wrong threshold. Test coverage cannot see that, so the answers get graded instead of the code.

    64 cases across 12 categories. 9 , every one a pure function, because a grader that needs a model to decide cannot gate a safety property: it fails the same way as the thing it grades. The tiers below are not an inventory of what goes wrong. They are the standing conditions under which an answer, and later the record of how it was used, can be trusted.

    Acceptance tiers, thresholds and what each tier covers
    TierThresholdWhat it covers
    safety100%no budgetinvented figures, refusal followed by a guess, contradicting the procedure, manufactured consensus
    trust95%named attribution, procedure cited first
    form90%wording and closing discipline

    One run scored 52 out of 53 and was declared a failure, because the single miss was in tier 1.

    08What it is for
    The vision

    What it is for.

    Accompanied autonomy

    Lore does not shorten certification and does not sign anything. It shortens the distance between being certified and being sure.

    A junior works at the edge of their competence when the net is there, and waits when it is not. Waiting is the cost: the escalation raised out of caution, the interesting task quietly given to someone else, the question not asked because asking twice in an hour has a price. Competence grows by doing things slightly too hard, which only happens when a mistake gets caught.

    Target: cut time-to-autonomy on an engine type by 30%. The lever is not working faster. It is removing the wait between a question and the person who already answered it.

    Three modes, one circuit

    Capture, query and log are not three features. They are one circuit, and the third decides whether the other two are worth anything.

    A junior asks. Lore answers with the procedure, the senior's judgement, the aircraft's history. Then he logs what he actually did: the reading, the conditions, the call he made. That log is not an archive entry. It is evidence about the knowledge that was just used.

    Marc's observation, acted on once, is one technician's note. Acted on twelve times across four winters with the same outcome, it is something the shop knows. And when an outcome contradicts it, the vibration that did escalate at 2.4 NU, that has to surface too, rather than sit buried under twelve confirmations.

    Knowledge that is only ever read decays. Knowledge that is used and measured gets sharper, and the reason to trust it stops being who said it and becomes what happened next.

    The honest state

    The circuit is open today. All three modes work; nothing yet weights an observation by what happened after it was used.

    What already exists is the guardrail. Lore will not state that technicians agree unless two or more retrieved sources actually say so, and that rule is held by a deterministic rather than by asking the model nicely. The rule that will govern the counting is in place before the counting. Most products do it the other way round and discover later that they manufactured a consensus.

    An aircraft keeps its own reasoning

    Not the maintenance log, which records what was done, but the reasoning around it: why this airframe was left alone at 2.4 NU in January and escalated at 2.9 in March. Tail number as the unit of memory, rather than one technician's recollection.

    What it opens elsewhere

    Aviation is the most demanding case, not the only one. Wherever expertise is oral, specific to one machine, and held by a generation on its way out, the same loss occurs: energy and nuclear, rail, process industry, shipping, utilities, and operating theatres where technique passes by apprenticeship.

    That portability is not a projection. Aetherix solves the same loss in hospitality: a manager leaves and takes what they knew about the property. Same memory architecture, two industries, two levels of tolerance for being wrong. Built twice, not argued once.

    09Where it stands

    Where it stands, honestly.

    This is a prototype, not production software.

    • 01Synthetic procedures and mock aircraft data. No real EASA-regulated documents, no real tail numbers, no real technician records.
    • 02No authentication on the API routes.
    • 03Contradiction between two experts is now tested, a second expert was written into the case set specifically to disagree with the first, but it is not a shipped detection feature.
    • 04Multi-source confidence scoring remains design intent.
    • 05No runtime observability. If the model degrades in production, nothing would notice before a user did.

    The last two lines are the honest ones, and they are why the evaluation write-up exists.
